I have spent a lot of time lately moving through northern hardwood forests and talking with people who manage them. One recurring reminder from that work is that good forestry depends on being able to look at the trees in front of you and know what they are: not only when the leaves are perfect and conveniently held at eye level, but when it is February, the bark is wet, the crown is forty feet away, and the tree has been doing something strange for the last 80 years.

That idea grew into a course.

The [Northern Hardwoods & Mixedwoods Dendrology and Silvics Intensive](/course/) is now live on Cochetopa. It is a ten-week, upper-division-style forestry course built around the tree species of the northern hardwood, Great Lakes–St. Lawrence, Acadian, Allegheny, boreal-transition, and associated mixedwood forests of northeastern North America.

It is self-paced, so “ten weeks” can mean ten serious weeks, a longer field-season project, or a slightly unreasonable weekend experiment. The expectations stay the same either way.

## This is primarily an identification course

The course covers 80 principal taxa, weighted toward the trees a forester is most likely to encounter across the Lake States, Northeast, eastern Canada, and northern Allegheny region. The common species receive the most repetition, while regional trees and realistic planted or invasive confusers appear where they actually create identification problems.

The central rule is that recognizing one photograph does not mean you know a species. A sugar maple has to remain a sugar maple when the evidence changes.

That means identification work from:

- foliage, including sun and shade leaves, imperfect material, and seasonal variation;
- young, intermediate, mature, weathered, wet, damaged, and lichen-covered bark;
- buds, twigs, leaf scars, pith, pubescence, and other dormant characters;
- seeds, samaras, acorns, nuts, cones, catkins, drupes, and persistent fruit;
- whole-tree form, crown architecture, regeneration habit, site, and associates; and
- disease, insects, injury, browse, and other conditions that make real trees less cooperative than herbarium sheets.

The public teaching and practice library contains more than a thousand photographs across those evidence types. Formal assessments draw from separately held examination material, so repeated image recognition cannot substitute for learning the tree.

## The difficult comparisons are taught together

The course spends a lot of time on confusion sets: sugar, black, and Norway maple; the northern ashes; paper, heart-leaved, and gray birch; four spruces plus Norway spruce; red, jack, and Scots pine; the red-oak and white-oak groups; elms; aspens and poplars; hickories; cherries; hornbeam and hophornbeam; native compound-leaved trees versus black locust and tree-of-heaven; and quite a few others.

The recurring questions are not only “What is it?” They are also: What is the nearest plausible alternative? What visible character separates them? What additional view would you request? And is the photograph actually good enough to support a species-level answer?

Knowing when the evidence is insufficient is part of reliable field forestry. Otherwise, every partial twig or distant crown becomes a confident guess.

## Silvics stays attached to the tree

Identification is the largest part of the course, but every principal species also carries a concise silvics capsule: shade tolerance, moisture and site tendencies, geographic affinity, regeneration, succession, disturbance response, longevity, browse, important insects and diseases, and management implications for field work.

Scientific names are cumulative from the beginning. So are previous mistakes.

The course keeps a mastery record by species and evidence type, schedules weak material more frequently, and maintains an Error Ledger that distinguishes not knowing a tree from confusing two known trees, missing the scientific name, getting the ecology wrong, or being much more confident than the evidence justified. Weekly practicals, a midterm, nomenclature and silvics work, condition identification, and a final examination all contribute to a running grade.

## Taking the course

You can [open the course here](/course/) and look through the syllabus and first module. An email address is enough to create an account; sign-in uses an emailed link rather than a password. Progress, grades, mastery, and remediation follow the learner between devices. Encouragement emails are optional and remain off unless the learner turns them on.

This is the first public release, and it is intentionally more demanding than a casual tree-identification quiz. It is meant to reward durable recognition, careful discrimination, and the ability to make a defensible call from whatever evidence a tree happens to give you.

The goal is to become very hard to fool in the woods, even if the individual questions never start to feel easy.
